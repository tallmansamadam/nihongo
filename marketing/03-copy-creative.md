# Nihongo Reader — Copy & Creative Kit

**Product truth (do not exceed):** Free, open-source Japanese-learning app for serious learners. Tap any kanji to drill down: readings, radical, recursive grapheme components, animated stroke order, mnemonic, pronunciation. Graded stories/articles/folktales with furigana; JLPT N5–N1 practice tests; flashcards; annotated classic songs; voice reader; offline; no ads, no account, no streaks. Windows + Android (sideload). GitHub: github.com/tallmansamadam/nihongo

**Voice:** Confident, warm, a little wry. Respect the learner's intelligence. The enemy is shallow gamification and streak-anxiety — never a company or its people. No anti-AI messaging. No invented stats or testimonials.

## 4. Short-video concepts (15–30s, TikTok/Shorts, 9:16)

### A. "All the Way Down" — the decomposition hero (15–20s)
The tagline, filmed. Screen recording, macro framing, satisfying mechanical sound design (soft clicks, one koto pluck per split).

- **0–2s:** Extreme close-up of a story page in-app. A thumb taps 語 mid-sentence. On-screen text: "Tap any kanji."
- **2–5s:** Popover slides up: readings, mnemonic. Beat of stillness.
- **5–11s:** The character splits: 言 + 五 + 口 — then 言 splits again. Components cascade down the screen like a mobile unfolding. Text: "Any kanji. All the way down."
- **11–15s:** Stroke order animates, one confident brush line at a time, synced to a slow drum tap.
- **15–20s:** Cut to cream end card in the retro splash style: sunburst behind the wordmark, "Free · Open-source · Offline," GitHub URL. No voiceover needed; works muted with captions.

Fully animated in the splash-screen aesthetic. Voiceover: warm, mid-century airline-announcer cadence, played straight until the last line.

## 5. Creative direction: one campaign, one look

Everything ships in the splash-screen's world — a 50s/60s Japan travel poster that happens to advertise software.

**Palette.** Three colors, sampled directly from the splash screen: cream is the ground (backgrounds, negative space — be generous with it), vermilion is the single accent (CTAs, stamps, the sunburst, one accent per layout, no more), indigo does the work (body text, line art, the plane, the map). If a layout needs a fourth color, the layout is wrong.

**Type feel.** Display: a warm retro geometric sans with slight letterpress irregularity — think mid-century airline poster, not vaporwave. Japanese display text (日本へ行こう!) gets brush-adjacent or classic gothic weight and is a design element, always paired with translation in small caps nearby. Body: clean humanist sans. Never distress body text; distress is for stamps only.

**Layout motifs (the campaign's grammar).**
- **The sunburst:** background radiance behind any hero object — wordmark, phone, kanji. Rays in vermilion on cream, or cream on indigo for dark variants.
- **The flight path:** the dotted arc from the splash's prop plane becomes the universal connector — underlines, list leaders, progress lines, section dividers. Anything that moves in motion graphics travels along an arc, never a straight line.
- **Stamps and luggage labels:** feature callouts, prices ("FREE," stamped, slightly askew), and badges live in luggage-label and passport-stamp frames. "Open-source" reads beautifully as a customs stamp.
- **The map:** the top-down Japan silhouette anchors wide layouts; screenshots and features pin to it like destinations.

**Screenshots.** Never raw. Frame app captures inside a ticket, a postcard, or a device outline drawn in indigo line art, sitting on cream with a sunburst or flight path behind. Add uniform subtle paper grain to every deliverable — same grain file everywhere; it's the campaign's texture signature.

**Thumbnails (YouTube/Shorts).** One hero object on cream + sunburst + one stamp + ≤4 words. Rotate the hero (a kanji, the plane, the phone) but never the structure. A row of our thumbnails should look like a poster series from the same print shop.

**Tone check.** The look is nostalgic; the copy never is. Wry, modern sentences against period visuals is the whole joke. If a line would sound right in an actual 1950s ad, rewrite it.

## 6. Launch story post (Reddit r/LearnJapanese / HN — dev's voice)

**Title:** I built a free, open-source Japanese reading app where you can tap any kanji and take it apart

I've been learning Japanese for a while, and the wall I kept hitting wasn't vocabulary — it was reading. The gamified apps got me started, but somewhere past the basics they stopped being hard while Japanese very much did not. What I wanted was simple: read real things, and when I hit a kanji I didn't know, take it apart on the spot.

So I built Nihongo Reader. Tap any kanji in any text and you get its readings, its radical, its components — recursively, so you can keep drilling into the components of the components — plus animated stroke order, a mnemonic, and pronunciation. Around that core: graded stories, articles, and folktales with furigana, JLPT practice tests from N5 to N1, flashcards, annotated classic Japanese songs, and a voice reader. Everything works offline.

Things it doesn't have, on purpose: ads, accounts, streaks, or a mascot with feelings about your attendance. It's free and open-source, and it stays that way.

Currently on Windows and Android — Android is sideload-only for now, which I know is a hurdle; proper distribution is on the list. If you try it, I'd genuinely like to know where it falls short, especially from people prepping for the JLPT or reading their first native material.

GitHub: github.com/tallmansamadam/nihongo
